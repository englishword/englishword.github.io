In the late 19th century, in order to overcome the disorder of the then-prevailing phonetic transcription schemes, Western linguistics discussed and developed a phonetic scheme — the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA). The "international" of IPA refers to its being usable for explaining many languages; it does not mean it is "international" specifically with regard to English. For the sole purpose of memorizing words, the shortcomings of the IPA method are evident:

- It reduces different letter combinations that share the same sound to a single phonetic symbol, which easily leads to spelling errors.
- Its introduction disrupts the natural order of phonics thinking and severs the link between a word's sound and its form.
- It leads learners to abandon the more important pronunciation rules.

China currently relies mainly on IPA to explain English speech sounds; this approach is not a good method for second-language learners memorizing vocabulary.

As the table shows, unlike the IPA — which invents new characters — this phoneme notation unifies sound and form. The key to memorizing English words is to know this table by heart and pronounce every word accurately.
